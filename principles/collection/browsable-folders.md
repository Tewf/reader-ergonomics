# Browsable folders

**Claim:** a collection is shallow and broad: two levels rather than three, and folders a reader can scan, of at least 3 and at most about 32 entries.

- **Grade:** supported with caveat; the evidence is from menus and web pages, applied to folders by analogy.
- **Evidence:** [file structure](../../evidence/file-structure/review.md), [against](../../evidence/file-structure/against.md) (navigation).
- **Check:** `tree-shape` (exact): too many entries fails, too few asks a question, too deep warns.
- **Reader styles:** none override it.

## What it asks

- Prefer breadth to depth: a third level is the costly one.
- A folder of 1 or 2 entries folds into its parent, unless it is one record of a collection (a job, a chapter, a topic).
- Past about 32 entries, group by purpose. A folder reached through an index file, and read rather than browsed, may hold more.
- The entries' names carry the scent ([names predict content](names-predict-content.md)).

## Why

Two levels of 16 by 32 took 36 s, 32 by 16 took 46 s, and three levels of 8 took 58 s, among three web sites of 512
items each, with 19 participants. The two two-level layouts did not differ significantly, and only the three-level one
was slower (Larson 1998). Among 64 menu targets, two levels of eight beat both deeper and flatter menus (Miller 1981).
Needless depth costs more than breadth.

## Limits

The studies timed search for a named target through category labels. Developers mostly reach code by search, about
twelve queries a day (Sadowski 2015), and spend about a third of their time navigating (Ko 2006). An optimum of 8 to 16
items per level, often quoted, is not what these studies found.
