# Draw relations

**Claim:** a relation, a set, a quantity or a process is shown as a figure placed with the words that explain it, not as words alone.

- **Grade:** measured.
- **Evidence:** [learning science](../../evidence/learning-science/review.md) (strand 5), [typography and layout](../../evidence/typography-and-layout/review.md) (strand 6).
- **Check:** `figures-present` (judgment): a long text with no figure or table is asked about.
- **Reader styles:** strengthened by [visual-relational](../../reader-styles/visual-relational.md); replaced by an edge list or table for an [LLM reader](../../reader-styles/llm-reader.md).

## What it asks

- A relation between things is a graph; a set or a partition is regions; a quantity is length or position; a process is frames the reader steps through.
- A table when the reader looks up symbols; a graph when the reader compares shapes or trends (Vessey 1991).
- Position and length before angle, area or colour, which are judged less accurately (Cleveland 1984).
- No decorative pictures: seductive details have a small negative effect (Sundararajan 2020).

## Why

A diagram puts related items next to each other, so the reader neither searches nor infers what the page could show
(Larkin & Simon 1987). Words with pictures beat words alone across the multimedia literature (Mayer 2020). Building
a concept map beat activities without one, g = 0.82, and studying a map beat them by 0.37 (Nesbit 2006); in
the North American studies alone, building gave 0.47.

## Limits

Animation adds little over static frames unless the change itself is what is learned (Höffler 2007; Ploetzner 2020).
Translating between two representations is itself hard and needs support (Ainsworth 2006).
