# Worked examples with subgoals

**Claim:** a method is taught by a solved example whose steps are named by what they achieve, then faded: the next examples leave more steps to the reader.

- **Grade:** measured, for novices; reversed for experts.
- **Evidence:** [learning science](../../evidence/learning-science/review.md) (strand 3).
- **Check:** `shape-conformance` (exact, for documents declared as reports in config): the plan and its steps are present; whether the steps name goals is judgment.
- **Reader styles:** strengthened by [novice](../../reader-styles/novice.md); relaxed by [expert](../../reader-styles/expert.md).

## What it asks

- The first problem of a kind is solved in full, every step titled by its goal ("Isolate the variable"), not by the operation it performs.
- The next leaves its last steps to the reader; a later one is a whole problem, with the answer folded.
- Varied examples of one method, rather than several of the same surface.
- Correct examples first; an erroneous one only with a prompt to find the error.

## Why

Worked examples beat problem solving in mathematics by about 18 percentile points (Barbieri 2023). Removing steps one by
one beats jumping to whole problems (Renkl 2004). Subgoal labels transfer to new problems, more when they appear in
both the text and the example (Catrambone 1998; Margulieux 2016).

## Limits

The benefit reverses with expertise: novices gain from more guidance, d = +0.51, advanced learners from less,
d = -0.43 (Tetzlaff 2025). Once practice succeeds, testing beats more examples (Yeo 2019).
