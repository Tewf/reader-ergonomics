# Define before use

**Claim:** every term and symbol is defined at or before its first use, premises come in the order they are needed, and each thing keeps one name.

- **Grade:** supported with caveat.
- **Evidence:** [psycholinguistics](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/review.md) (strand 4), [learning science](../../evidence/learning-science/review.md) (strand 3), [LLM readers](../../evidence/llm-readers/).
- **Check:** `defined-before-use` (heuristic): a bold term with a definition cue, used earlier in the same file.
- **Reader styles:** strengthened by [novice](../../reader-styles/novice.md), [visual-relational](../../reader-styles/visual-relational.md) and [LLM reader](../../reader-styles/llm-reader.md); relaxed by [expert](../../reader-styles/expert.md).

## What it asks

- The term in bold where it is defined, with its definition on the same line; afterwards, the same word every time.
- A symbol is named in words in the sentence before the formula that uses it.
- A term defined in another file is linked at its first use here.

## Why

- A reader holds a few propositions at a time and links each new sentence to them by a shared argument. When
  nothing in the buffer matches, they search memory, which is slow (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978).
- Revising a text where a comprehension model found such gaps improved learning, most for readers short of skill or
  knowledge (Britton & Gülgöz 1991; Gülgöz et al. 1999, secondary).
- Learning the names and parts before the lesson helped transfer in all three experiments and retention in two
  (Mayer 2002).
- Language models reason better when premises come in the order they are used (see
  [LLM readers](../../evidence/llm-readers/)).

## Limits

Some high-knowledge readers learn more from a text that leaves links to infer (McNamara et al. 1996b), though not the
skilled ones (O'Reilly & McNamara 2007).

A term the reader already knows needs no definition. Across 60 studies, assistance helped novices and hurt advanced
learners (Tetzlaff 2025, a meta-analysis, measured; Kalyuga 2003 first reviewed the effect). The studies are mostly in
science and mathematics, the evidence is weakest for humanities and language, and the authors advise giving assistance when in doubt.

Closer to definitions, explanatory notes built into a text helped readers with low language competence and slowed
more knowledgeable ones (one study, reviewed in Kalyuga 2003). So with an unknown reader, define.

Practice agrees: Google's style guide exempts well-known terms such as API (Google 2026b, convention).
Which terms are known depends on the reader, so the reader style decides.

A tutorial that withholds explanation, as the Diátaxis documentation framework advises, links the explanation instead
(Procida 2026, convention).
