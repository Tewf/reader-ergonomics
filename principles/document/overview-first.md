# Overview first

**Claim:** a document opens with what it is about and how it is organised (its parts, in a few lines or a simple map) before any detail.

- **Grade:** measured.
- **Evidence:** [reading order](../../evidence/reading-order/review.md) (strands 1 and 2).
- **Check:** `overview-first` (heuristic): a long document's title is followed by an opening, not by another heading; whether the opening is an overview is judgment.
- **Reader styles:** strengthened by [novice](../../reader-styles/novice.md) and [LLM reader](../../reader-styles/llm-reader.md).

## What it asks

- The first lines say what the text is for and who it is for.
- Then the parts, three to five, in the order they come: a short list, an outline or a small map.
- When only part of the text matters to a reader, say which part, and for what goal.

## Why

A graphic organizer given with the text raised memory, g = 0.70, and comprehension, 0.53 (Ponce 2025). A topic given
before a passage raises comprehension and recall; the same topic given after it does not (Bransford & Johnson 1972).
Telling readers what is relevant raises learning of that part at no extra reading time (McCrudden 2007).

## Limits

The classic advance organizer has a small effect on immediate tests, d = 0.21 (Luiten 1980, secondary), and 0.66 on delayed tests (Stone 1983, secondary).
Mayer 1979 predicts it helps most on unfamiliar material. A network map did worse than a hierarchical one, most for readers with little prior knowledge, so a map should follow the domain's own hierarchy (Amadieu 2009). Stating
what matters lowers learning of what was not named (Klauer 1984).

Overview first is not importance first: the evidence above is for organizers, which announce the parts. Putting the
most important fact first, as news does, is a different order. Its few direct tests favour narrative order or find
no difference (Nildam 1998, a master's thesis; Sternadori 2008, a doctoral thesis, not read). So importance first
has no support yet, and is not refuted either.

In two experiments with Dutch readers, bad-news letters and e-mails that gave the explanation before the decision
were rated clearer, and the decision more accepted (Jansen 2010, abstract only). One culture, so a limit to keep in
mind, not a rule.
