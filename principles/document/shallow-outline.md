# Shallow outline

**Claim:** what matters most sits at the top of a document's hierarchy, and the outline stays shallow: at most three levels of headings.

- **Grade:** measured for recall of top-level ideas; the three-level limit is a convention.
- **Evidence:** [psycholinguistics](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/review.md) (strand 5), [against](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/against.md).
- **Check:** `outline-depth` (exact count; warns past level 3); whether the top levels carry the main claims is judgment.
- **Reader styles:** none override it.

## What it asks

- The main claims are top-level: headings or the first sentence of each part. Detail sits below them.
- Three heading levels at most. A fourth level is a sign that the part is a document of its own.
- Headings that name their content, so the outline alone gives the gist ([signal the structure](../page/signal-the-structure.md)).

## Why

- Idea units high in a text's content structure are recalled better than low ones, the **levels effect** (Meyer &
  McConkie 1973; Meyer 1975, secondary).
- Readers slow down more at a changed word high in the hierarchy than low in it (Wu et al. 2016).
- Readers who follow the top-level structure comprehend better (Meyer et al. 1980), and adults who attend to headings
  write the best summaries (Hyönä et al. 2002).
- The tree of a text can be made explicit: Rhetorical Structure Theory describes it as nucleus and satellite spans
  (Mann & Thompson 1988).

## Limits

The levels effect depends on the reader's skill (Meyer et al. 1980). The three-level limit is not measured; it follows
from the effect and from [browsable folders](../collection/browsable-folders.md).
