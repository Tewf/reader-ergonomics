# Short dependencies

**Claim:** words that depend on each other stay close: a subject near its verb, a modifier next to what it modifies, a pronoun near what it refers to.

- **Grade:** measured in controlled sentences; supported with caveat in natural reading; a pronoun
  near what it refers to is applied by extension.
- **Evidence:** [psycholinguistics](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/review.md) (strand 2), [against](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/against.md).
- **Check:** `dependency-length` (syntax, weekly): no word more than 12 words from the word it depends on; `long-sentences` on every push.
- **Reader styles:** none override it.

## What it asks

- No long clause between a subject and its verb; move it after the verb or into its own sentence.
- Long lists and qualifications go after the main clause, not before it.

## Why

Integration difficulty grows with the new discourse referents between a word and its head (Grodner & Gibson 2005).
Dependency locality theory predicts this (Gibson 1998). Every one of 37 languages keeps its dependencies shorter than
random reorderings would (Futrell et al. 2015).

## Limits

In an eye-tracking corpus the cost held for nouns and not for words in general (Demberg & Keller 2008). Interference
between similar words, not distance itself, may carry the cost (Lewis & Vasishth 2005). The studies measure
syntactic dependencies; a pronoun and what it refers to are not one, so that clause is applied by extension. The
centering studies here (Gordon 1993) test which word a pronoun refers to, not its distance.
