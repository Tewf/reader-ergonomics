# Shallow nesting

**Claim:** a sentence puts at most one clause inside another; with two nested clauses, readers already misread.

- **Grade:** measured.
- **Evidence:** [psycholinguistics](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/review.md) (strand 3), [against](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/against.md).
- **Check:** `clause-nesting` (syntax, weekly): no sentence nests more than two clauses.
- **Reader styles:** strengthened by [novice](../../reader-styles/novice.md); relaxed by [LLM reader](../../reader-styles/llm-reader.md).

## What it asks

- One embedded clause at a time; a second becomes its own sentence.
- Prefer a clause at the end of a sentence to one in its middle.

## Why

With two nested relative clauses, English readers rate a sentence missing its middle verb as acceptable as the
complete one (Gibson & Thomas 1999). Difficulty comes from finite memory, not from grammar (Miller & Chomsky 1963).
In seven European languages, written text never nests past three clauses, and speech hardly nests at all (Karlsson 2007).

## Limits

The limit depends on the language: German readers are not fooled the way English readers are (Vasishth et al. 2010).
The corpus maximum counts what writers produced, not what readers can parse.
