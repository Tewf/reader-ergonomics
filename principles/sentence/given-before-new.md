# Given before new

**Claim:** a sentence starts from what the reader already has (the topic, or what the previous sentence ended on) and ends on what is new.

- **Grade:** supported with caveat.
- **Evidence:** [psycholinguistics](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/review.md) (strands 4 and 5), [against](../../evidence/psycholinguistics/against.md).
- **Check:** `sentence-links` (syntax, weekly): consecutive sentences share a noun or point back.
- **Reader styles:** none override it.

## What it asks

- Open a sentence with a word that links it to the one before; put the new information last, where the stress falls.
- Keep the same entity as the subject across consecutive sentences when they are about it.

## Why

A sentence is read faster when its given part has a direct antecedent, the **given-new strategy** (Haviland & Clark
1974, secondary). In five reading-time experiments, the entity linking a sentence to the previous one was read best as
the subject (Gordon et al. 1993). Gopen & Swan 1990 turned this into writing advice for scientists: topic first, stress
position last.

## Limits

The practitioner version was never tested as a whole (Gopen & Swan 1990 report no controlled study).
