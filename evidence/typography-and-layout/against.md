# Against

## Dyslexia fonts and folk rules

- Dyslexia-specific fonts: null effects in Rello 2013, Wery 2017 and Kuster 2018 (n = 170).
- Italic, underline and justified text are widely discouraged. Only italic has direct support (worse in Rello 2013). No source of 10 or more was read on justification; Akhmadeeva 2016 is below the quality bar: an example, not evidence.
- Sans serif is recommended for dyslexia; Bernard 2002 finds no speed difference for typical readers.
- The letter-spacing gains come from one research group (Zorzi 2012); replications by other groups were not reviewed here.

## Line length

- Speed and comprehension peak at different lengths (Dyson 2004), and preference follows neither.
- The 66-character ideal is typographic craft tradition, not a measured optimum.
- CSS ch is not the same as characters per line.

## Polarity

- Piepenbrock 2013 favours dark-on-light; Dobres 2017 finds no daytime difference. At night white on black was clearly less legible (negative polarity disadvantage), so the night benefit belongs to dark text on light.

## Layout and design

- Tufte's data-ink rule: intuition; Bateman 2010 found embellishment harmless for accuracy.
- F-pattern: a description of scanning on cluttered pages, not a law for structured text.
- Chunking advice rests on Miller 1956 and anecdote.
- Syntax highlighting: no comprehension effect (Hannebauer 2018).
- Collapsed sections: reduce clutter but hide content (Wang 2023).

## Limits of the evidence base

- Many studies use short passages, small samples or screens of the 2000s.
- Practitioner sources (Nielsen Norman Group, Bringhurst, BDA) give guidance, not controlled trials.

## Practice that contradicts a principle

- Readable measure says 45 to 75 characters. USWDS 2026 allows up to 90, and WCAG 1.4.8 (AAA) offers 80. Neither cites a study.
- Sufficient contrast states one ratio. APCA 2026 says the threshold depends on size and weight; W3C has not adopted it.
- The layer-cake claims of Nielsen Norman 2019 (below the quality bar: an example, not evidence) rest on what the page shows: one 9-participant heatmap and no measured effect; the F-pattern is descriptive (Nielsen 2006).
